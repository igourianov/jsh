A great engineer understands value and makes decisions, not just executes tasks. They have strong opinions, can defend them, and prioritize code readability over cleverness. They know clean code means maintainability, not obscure syntax tricks. Most importantly, they understand the value they provide to the stakeholders and able to balance pragmatism with principle - shipping features, fixing bugs, and designing systems as needed.

Problem: Multiple teams, all using trunk-based development with shared test environments. Result: 30% deployment failure rate, constant collisions, daily firefighting.

I proposed a structured branching model—feature branches, environment isolation, merge gates. Adding process.

Significant pushback: My engineers saw bureaucracy. Other EMs accused me of creating bottlenecks.

I'd done the math: we lost more time to deployment failures than we'd ever lose to process. Piloted with one team for two weeks, measured results, rolled out despite resistance.

Result: 90% fewer deployment failures. Teams worked independently. Iteration speed increased because we stopped firefighting.

Lesson: Sometimes courage means adding the right structure when "move fast and break things" is the popular answer.
