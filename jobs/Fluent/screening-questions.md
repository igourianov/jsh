# Screening Questions

## Fluent runs an AI Champions Program and expects engineering leaders to drive AI adoption. How have you introduced AI-assisted development practices (e.g., Copilot, Cursor, Claude) into an engineering team? What measurable impact did it have on velocity or quality, and how did you handle resistance from engineers who were skeptical?

I'm actively driving AI adoption in my own engineering practice using Claude Code as my primary AI-assisted development tool, and I've developed a phased adoption strategy for engineering organizations based on my experience. My approach starts with low-risk, high-visibility wins: implementing AI-powered code reviews on pull requests in a suggestions-only mode first to build developer trust, then gradually enabling automated approvals for high-confidence cases, targeting a 70-80% reduction in human PR review overhead.
For broader adoption, I advocate building a shared repository of curated prompts and reusable AI skills that developers can start with on trivial tasks (commit workflows, boilerplate generation) before progressing to more complex agentic workflows with planner, coder, and reviewer agents.
To handle skepticism, I've found the key is removing pressure: start AI as advisory-only so engineers see value before any process changes, run show-and-tell sessions where early adopters demo real productivity gains, and let developers opt in through curiosity rather than mandates.
The measurable impact I've seen in my own workflow is significant - tasks that previously took hours (research, scaffolding, refactoring) now take minutes - and the strategy is designed to scale that across a team incrementally rather than through a disruptive big-bang rollout.

## Our ad-serving platform processes millions of daily events through Kafka with sub-100ms latency requirements. Describe a system you've built or operated that had strict availability SLAs on an event-driven architecture. Walk me through how you handled partition strategy, consumer group scaling, exactly-once vs. at-least-once trade-offs, and what your incident response playbook looked like when the pipeline fell behind.

At Dayforce, I managed the platform team responsible for core services on a multi-tenant enterprise SaaS platform serving Fortune 500 customers with strict availability SLAs. Our architecture used Kafka and Redis for inter-service communication as part of the monolith-to-microservices decomposition I led. My role was on the engineering management side: I owned the technical strategy, staffed the team with senior/staff engineers who made the low-level architecture decisions, and was accountable for uptime and incident response. I was directly involved in system design reviews where we evaluated trade-offs like at-least-once delivery (our default, with idempotent consumers) versus the throughput cost of exactly-once semantics. For incident response, I established a playbook that included Prometheus/Grafana dashboards for consumer lag monitoring, defined escalation paths, and led blameless post-incident reviews to turn outages into process improvements.
I want to be transparent: I haven't personally tuned Kafka partition strategies at the level your ad-serving platform demands, but I've led the teams that made those decisions, I understand the trade-offs well enough to evaluate proposals and challenge assumptions, and I ramp quickly on operational depth when I'm closer to the system.
